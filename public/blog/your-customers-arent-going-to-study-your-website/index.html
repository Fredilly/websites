The business of being understood · Website design

Your customers aren't going to study your website.

They came to solve a problem. Don't give them another one.

Article6 Studio · November 13, 2026 · 5-minute read

Imagine you need someone to fix the air conditioning in your office. It’s getting hot, people are complaining, and you’ve found three companies that might be able to help.

The first website opens with a drone shot of a glass tower. Underneath it says, “Innovative solutions for tomorrow’s world.” There’s a photo of a handshake. Then a paragraph about excellence.

Somewhere, perhaps, is the answer to your actual question: Can you come this week and fix the air conditioning?

The second website says: “Commercial air-conditioning repair in Dubai. Emergency callouts available. Tell us the problem and we’ll respond with the next available appointment.”

Which company would you contact first?

Not because the second company necessarily does better work. You have no way of knowing that yet. But it has done something useful before asking for your trust: it has made itself easy to understand.

The problem isn’t that people have short attention spans

Business owners are often told to make their websites “more engaging.” So they add animations, moving headlines, pop-ups, a carousel of testimonials and a cheerful chatbot that introduces itself before anyone has asked a question.

Sometimes those things have a place. But the customer didn't arrive looking to be entertained. They arrived with a job to do.

They want to know whether you deal with their kind of problem, whether you operate where they are, whether you seem credible and what happens if they contact you.

If those answers require detective work, the customer is doing your marketing for you.

Every question your website refuses to answer becomes work for your customer.

And the competition is usually one browser tab away.

The most expensive sentence on a website is the one that says nothing

“We deliver bespoke, world-class solutions that empower businesses to unlock their potential.”

You've seen some version of that sentence a hundred times. It sounds respectable. It also describes almost every consulting firm, software company, cleaning service and marketing agency on the internet.

Try replacing it with an answer that risks being specific.

Instead of “End-to-end digital transformation,” try “We replace manual stock records with a simple system your shop staff can use on a phone.”

Instead of “Premium property solutions,” try “We manage apartment rentals for owners who live outside Dubai.”

These aren't fancy lines. That's the point. A stranger can decide, immediately, whether the offer is for them.

What an ordinary visitor needs in the first minute

Open your own website in a private browser window. Pretend you don't know the business. Then look for five answers:

  1. What exactly do you sell? Name the service or product, not the category of ambition it belongs to.
  2. Who is it for? A restaurant owner and a multinational procurement team have different problems.
  3. Where do you work? If location matters, say it without making people search your footer.
  4. Why should I believe you? Show real work, a clear process, relevant experience or evidence. Don't manufacture awards and superlatives.
  5. What happens next? “Get in touch” is vague. “Tell us about the site you need and we’ll discuss the scope” is an actual next step.

You don't need a 40-page website to answer those questions. You need the courage to remove the things competing with the answers.

Now there's another reader to consider

People aren't the only ones trying to understand your business. Search engines and AI-powered answer systems also have to work out what you do from what your website makes available.

They don't need a secret “AI optimization trick” nearly as much as they need readable pages, consistent company information, clear services and claims that can be checked.

A paragraph that tells a customer exactly what you offer is useful for a person. It is also easier for machines to interpret than a slogan that could belong to anybody.

That doesn't guarantee you'll appear in an AI answer. Nobody serious can promise that. But making your business less ambiguous is a sensible place to begin, regardless of what search looks like next year.

Before you redesign anything

Ask someone who has never visited your site to look at the homepage for thirty seconds. Close the tab, then ask them what your business does, who you serve and how they would contact you.

Don't explain. Don't help. Write down what they remember.

If their answers are wrong, your first problem probably isn't the colour palette or the font. It's the story the page is telling.

Fix that story before spending money making it move.
